# Sleep Hygiene and Insomnia Management

## Why Sleep Matters for Mental Health
Sleep and mental health are bidirectionally linked:
- Poor sleep worsens depression, anxiety, and irritability
- Depression and anxiety disrupt sleep
- 1 in 3 people with insomnia develops depression if untreated
- During deep sleep, the brain clears out toxins and consolidates emotional memories

## Sleep Architecture
A healthy night has 4–6 sleep cycles (~90 minutes each):
- **N1**: Light sleep, transition
- **N2**: Real sleep, heart rate slows, body temperature drops
- **N3 (Deep sleep)**: Physical restoration, immune function, memory consolidation
- **REM**: Emotional processing, dream sleep, creativity

Alcohol and late eating suppress N3 and REM — meaning you can sleep 8 hours and still feel unrestored.

## Common Causes of Sleep Problems
- Stress and worry (racing thoughts in bed)
- Irregular sleep schedule (different times on weekends)
- Excessive blue light from screens
- Caffeine after noon
- Physical discomfort: pain, temperature
- Alcohol (disrupts sleep architecture)
- Napping more than 20 minutes during the day
- Anxiety about sleep itself (sleep anxiety)

## Evidence-Based Sleep Strategies

### Stimulus Control (Most Effective Single Technique)
Train your brain to associate bed with sleep only:
- Use your bed ONLY for sleep (and sex). No phone, TV, eating, or working in bed.
- If you can't sleep after 20 minutes, get up and do something calm in dim light until sleepy.
- Return to bed only when actually sleepy.
- Repeat as needed — avoid lying awake in bed.

### Sleep Restriction (CBT-I)
Paradoxically, spending less time in bed builds sleep pressure and improves sleep quality:
- Calculate average actual sleep time (e.g., 5 hours)
- Go to bed 5 hours before wake time for 1 week
- Gradually extend by 15 minutes when sleep efficiency improves above 85%

### Sleep Hygiene Basics
- **Consistent schedule**: Same bedtime AND wake time 7 days a week — even weekends
- **Light exposure**: Bright sunlight within 1 hour of waking (regulates circadian rhythm)
- **No screens**: 30–60 minutes before bed. Blue light suppresses melatonin by up to 3 hours.
- **Cool room**: 18–20°C (65–68°F) is optimal. Body needs to cool to enter sleep.
- **No caffeine after noon**: Caffeine half-life is 5–7 hours. A 2pm coffee still has half its caffeine at 9pm.
- **No alcohol**: Alcohol helps you fall asleep but fragments sleep badly in the second half.
- **Exercise**: Regular exercise improves sleep quality — but avoid vigorous exercise 2 hours before bed.

### Wind-Down Routine (60 minutes before bed)
1. Dim all lights in your home
2. Finish eating at least 2–3 hours before bed
3. Gentle stretch or progressive muscle relaxation
4. Write tomorrow's 3 priorities and a gratitude list (offloads mental churn)
5. Read fiction or do a calm activity (crosswords, light conversation)
6. In bed: 4-7-8 breathing until drowsy

### Managing Racing Thoughts at Night
- **Worry journal**: Write worries before bed. Your brain stops rehearsing them.
- **Scheduled worry time**: 15 minutes earlier in the evening to write worries, not in bed.
- **Cognitive defusion**: "I'm having the thought that X" — distance yourself from the thought.
- **Body scan**: Move attention systematically through the body (see exercise guide).

### Napping Rules
- If you must nap: maximum 20 minutes, before 3pm
- Short naps restore alertness without disrupting night sleep
- Long naps (60–90 min) reduce sleep pressure and worsen nighttime sleep

## Sleep Myths
- "I can catch up on sleep over the weekend" — FALSE. Sleep debt isn't fully recovered, and irregular schedules harm circadian rhythm.
- "Alcohol helps me sleep" — FALSE. It helps you fall asleep but dramatically worsens sleep quality.
- "Watching TV helps me wind down" — FALSE. Screens increase alertness. Netflix is specifically engineered to keep you watching.
- "I only need 5 hours" — FALSE for almost everyone. Only 1–3% of people have the genetic mutation for this.

## When to Seek Professional Help
- Insomnia lasting more than 3 months
- Daytime functioning significantly impaired
- Suspected sleep apnea (loud snoring, gasping, partner reports you stop breathing)
- Restless legs, sleepwalking, or sleep paralysis
- Sleep problems combined with depression or anxiety
