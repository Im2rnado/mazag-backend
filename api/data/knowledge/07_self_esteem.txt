# Self-Esteem, Self-Compassion, and Negative Self-Talk

## What is Self-Esteem?
Self-esteem is the overall sense of worth and value you place on yourself. It's shaped by:
- Early experiences (parenting, school, peers)
- Cultural messages (about success, appearance, gender roles)
- Ongoing experiences (achievements, failures, relationship quality)
- The stories we tell ourselves

Low self-esteem is NOT a fixed trait — it can change with the right work.

## Signs of Low Self-Esteem
- Harsh self-criticism, even for small mistakes
- Difficulty accepting compliments ("They're just being nice")
- Comparing yourself unfavorably to others constantly
- Fear of failure holding you back from trying
- People-pleasing at the cost of your own needs
- Difficulty setting boundaries
- Feeling like a fraud (Impostor Syndrome)
- Believing you are fundamentally defective or unlovable
- Apologizing excessively

## The Inner Critic
Everyone has an "inner critic" voice — the running commentary of self-judgment. Common inner critic statements:
- "You're so stupid."
- "Why can't you be more like X?"
- "You don't deserve good things."
- "Everyone can see what a failure you are."

This voice often sounds like authority, so we believe it. But it is NOT truth — it's a habit, shaped by past experiences.

## Challenging Negative Self-Talk

### The Friend Test
Ask yourself: "Would I say this to a dear friend in this situation?"
If not, why do you say it to yourself?
Practice: Write what you'd say to a friend, then say that to yourself.

### Cognitive Defusion (ACT Technique)
Instead of: "I'm a failure."
Try: "I'm having the thought that I'm a failure."
The thought is not you. You are the one observing the thought.

### Evidence Examination (CBT)
"What evidence do I have that I'm a failure?"
"What evidence contradicts this?"
"What would a more accurate, balanced view be?"

### Naming the Critic
Give your inner critic a name and notice when it speaks. "There goes 'the judge' again." This creates distance and reduces its power.

### Positive Data Log
Keep a daily record of:
- Small things you did well
- Positive feedback you received (write it down, because you'll discount it otherwise)
- Moments you were kind, capable, or brave
Over time, this builds a more balanced self-view.

## Self-Compassion (Kristin Neff's Framework)
Self-compassion is NOT self-pity or making excuses. It is treating yourself with the same kindness you'd show a good friend.

Three components:
1. **Self-kindness**: Responding to your pain/mistakes with warmth, not harsh judgment
2. **Common humanity**: Recognizing your suffering is part of shared human experience — you are not alone
3. **Mindfulness**: Holding your experience in balanced awareness, not suppressing or over-identifying

### Self-Compassion Break (exercise)
When upset or self-critical, place your hand on your heart and say:
- "This is a moment of suffering." (mindfulness)
- "Suffering is part of life. I am not alone in this." (common humanity)
- "May I be kind to myself in this moment." (self-kindness)

### Self-Compassion vs Self-Esteem
Self-esteem goes up when things go well, down when they don't.
Self-compassion is stable — it's available even in failure and difficulty.
Self-compassion leads to greater resilience and less fear of failure.

## Building Healthy Self-Esteem

### Values Clarification
Low self-esteem is often based on other people's values — what THEY think you should be.
- Identify YOUR core values (honesty, creativity, connection, growth?)
- Build your life around those, not external standards

### Competence-Building
Self-esteem grows from doing hard things. Choose one area to work on consistently.
Success breeds confidence. Start small-sized challenges progressively.

### Reducing Comparison
"Comparison is the thief of joy." (Theodore Roosevelt)
- Social media shows highlight reels, not reality
- Everyone is struggling with something you can't see
- Compare yourself only to who you were last month

### Healthy Achievement
Do things because you find them meaningful, not for approval.
External validation is unreliable — you can't build lasting self-esteem on it.

## Impostor Syndrome
The persistent fear of being exposed as a "fraud," despite evidence of competence.
- Extremely common among high-achievers, students, and women
- Talk about it — you'll find most peers feel the same way
- Attribute your success to your own skills and efforts, not luck alone
- Keep a file of positive feedback and achievements to review when the feeling hits
