# Panic Attacks: Understanding and Coping

## What is a Panic Attack?
A panic attack is a sudden, intense surge of fear accompanied by severe physical symptoms. It typically peaks within 10 minutes and usually subsides within 20–30 minutes.

Key fact: Panic attacks are NOT dangerous. Despite feeling like you're dying, having a heart attack, or going crazy — panic attacks are not physically harmful.

## Panic Attack Symptoms
Physical:
- Racing or pounding heart (palpitations)
- Chest pain or tightness
- Shortness of breath or feeling of choking/smothering
- Dizziness, lightheadedness, or feeling faint
- Sweating, chills, or hot flashes
- Trembling or shaking
- Numbness or tingling (especially hands and face)
- Nausea or stomach upset

Psychological:
- Intense fear of dying or having a heart attack
- Fear of losing control or "going crazy"
- Feeling detached from yourself (depersonalization)
- Feeling the world is unreal (derealization)

## The Panic Cycle
1. Trigger (real or imagined threat, or no obvious trigger)
2. Physical sensation (e.g., slightly fast heartbeat)
3. Catastrophic thought ("I'm having a heart attack!")
4. Anxiety increases → more physical symptoms
5. More catastrophic thoughts → full panic attack

Understanding this cycle is key to breaking it.

## In the Moment: What to Do

### Remember: It Will Pass
Remind yourself: "This is a panic attack. It is temporary. It is not dangerous. It will pass."
A mantra can help: "I am safe. This is panic, not danger. It will be over soon."

### Controlled Breathing
Panic attacks involve hyperventilation, which worsens symptoms. Slow your breathing:
**4-7-8 Method**: Inhale 4 counts, hold 7, exhale 8.
**Box breathing**: Inhale 4, hold 4, exhale 4, hold 4.
Don't try to breathe deeply — breathe SLOWLY.

### Grounding (5-4-3-2-1)
Name: 5 things you see, 4 you can touch, 3 you hear, 2 you smell, 1 you taste.
This redirects attention from internal sensations to external reality.

### Cold Water
Splash cold water on your face or hold ice — activates the dive reflex, rapidly slowing heart rate.

### Don't Run
The urge to escape is strong. Staying (if safe) teaches your brain the situation isn't actually dangerous.

### Don't Fight the Panic
Trying to suppress panic often makes it worse. Instead: "I notice panic. I let it be here. It will pass."

## Longer-Term Strategies

### Interoceptive Exposure (most effective technique)
Deliberately induce mild panic sensations to reduce fear of them:
- Spinning in a chair (dizziness)
- Breathing through a thin straw (shortness of breath)
- Vigorous exercise (racing heart)
- Shaking head side to side (lightheadedness)

Doing these exercises repeatedly teaches your brain: "These sensations are uncomfortable, not dangerous." This dramatically reduces panic.

### Reduce Caffeine
Caffeine directly triggers panic attack symptoms. Gradually reduce to one cup per day maximum.

### Address Avoidance
Avoiding places/situations where you've panicked maintains panic disorder.
Gradual, systematic exposure to avoided places (with support) is essential for recovery.

### Lifestyle Foundations
- Regular aerobic exercise (reduces background anxiety significantly)
- Consistent sleep
- Mindfulness practice (builds tolerance for uncomfortable sensations)
- Reduce alcohol (alcohol increases anxiety rebound)

## What Panic is NOT
- NOT a sign you're going crazy
- NOT a heart attack (though get chest pain checked medically if first time)
- NOT something that will make you faint (blood pressure rises during panic, not falls)
- NOT caused by weakness or lack of willpower

## When to Seek Treatment
Panic Disorder (repeated panic attacks + worry about future attacks) responds very well to:
- **CBT** — specifically exposure therapy and interoceptive exposure
- **Medication** — SSRIs, SNRIs, or short-term benzodiazepines prescribed by a psychiatrist
- Combination of both

Most people with panic disorder can achieve significant reduction or complete elimination of panic attacks with proper treatment.

## Supporting Someone Having a Panic Attack
- Stay calm — your calm is contagious
- Speak slowly and quietly: "I'm here. You're safe. This will pass."
- Don't say "calm down" — help them breathe instead
- Don't dismiss it ("It's all in your head") — the physical sensations are real
- Don't call an ambulance unless they lose consciousness or have genuine cardiac risk factors
- After the attack: normalize it and encourage professional support
